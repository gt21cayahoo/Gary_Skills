# Weekly Lighting Intelligence Report — Editorial and Format Instructions

**Status:** Working draft — build collaboratively and confirm before producing the first edition  
**Purpose:** Define a portable, repeatable format for an ultra-brief weekly lighting-industry intelligence report.  
**Primary coverage authority:** `Coverage_Charter.md`

## 1. Report Objective

Create a clean, minimal weekly report that allows a reader to understand the most important developments in each market area in a few minutes.

The report should borrow the restrained, highly scannable character of the *Great Waters Gazette*, adapted to a professional market-intelligence audience:

- extremely short summaries;
- strong visual hierarchy;
- one principal story per section;
- one relevant image for the principal story;
- one-line briefs for all additional stories;
- a clickable original-source link for every referenced article, announcement, social-media post, study, or other item;
- at least three verified, category-correct stories in every section: one lead and at least two briefs;
- no filler or internal QA copy added merely to make a section look complete.

## 2. Report Structure

Begin directly with the first market section. Do not create an opening infographic, dashboard, snapshot, or separate summary page.

### Market Sections

1. **Architectural Indoor — Peerless + A-Light**
   - Cover Peerless and the integration of A-Light into Peerless.
   - A-Light is not monitored as an independent ongoing brand unless this direction changes.

2. **Performance Decorative — Eureka**
   - Cover design-driven decorative and architectural lighting relevant to Eureka.

3. **Outdoor Architectural — Luminis + Hydrel + Cyclone**
   - Cover Luminis, Hydrel, and Cyclone in one outdoor section.
   - Preserve their distinct strategic lenses: visible architectural form and coordinated families for Luminis; façade, surface, and high-performance illumination for Hydrel; municipal, streetscape, public-realm, and channel developments for Cyclone.

4. **Healthcare — Nightingale**
   - Cover healthcare and behavioral-health lighting relevant to Nightingale.

5. **Downlighting — Aculux + Gotham**
   - Cover high-end commercial/residential and integrator-channel developments relevant to Aculux, plus commercial architectural-downlighting developments relevant to Gotham.

6. **Customer + Design-Firm Input**
   - Cover meaningful signals from customers, architects, lighting designers, interior designers, landscape architects, healthcare designers, integrators, and other priority specifiers.

7. **Design Technology + Building Workflow**
   - Cover material changes in how buildings are conceived, analyzed, modeled, coordinated, documented, visualized, and delivered.
   - Prioritize AI-native design, generative and computational design, BIM evolution, interoperability, cloud collaboration, code and performance analysis, automated MEP/electrical design, and new architect–software workflows.
   - Focus on how these changes can alter whether, when, and by whom our products are discovered, evaluated, compared, specified, and selected.
   - Explain the potential consequence for product visibility, digital product data, BIM content, photometric and performance inputs, specification pathways, controls integration, collaboration, and relationships with architects and engineers.

## 3. Required Layout for Every Section

Use a restrained magazine-card layout rather than a linear article followed by a detached image gallery. The lead story should occupy one large bordered rectangle with its image integrated into the card. Secondary stories should appear as smaller modular rectangles, each pairing its ultra-short text with a directly relevant thumbnail when one is available. Maintain strong white space and avoid turning the page into a dense dashboard.

### A. Section Header

- Use a simple, prominent section title.
- Optionally include a very short descriptor naming the brands or market lens.

### B. Most Important Story

Place the single most important story at the top of the page.

Include:

1. **Headline** — concise and informative.
2. **Summary paragraph** — one short paragraph explaining what happened and why it matters to the section.
3. **Image** — extract or use a relevant image associated with the story.
   - For a new-product story, prefer the clearest official product image.
   - Otherwise, prefer an official project, application, event, or organization image that directly represents the story.
   - Include a short caption and source credit.
   - Link the image or adjacent source label to the original story when practical.
4. **Source link** — provide a clearly clickable link to the original source.

### C. Other Stories

Below the lead story, place the remaining relevant items in compact magazine-style story cards.

Publish at least two verified briefs so every section contains at least three real stories in total. If the current-week search is thin, expand methodically into the 30-day watch and then verified undated active signals, labeling each item honestly. Never count a category note, research-status statement, or other internal QA language as a story.

Each brief must contain:

- a very short headline or summary;
- the key relevance or implication when it can fit cleanly;
- a clickable link to the original source.
- a directly relevant thumbnail when the story has a usable product, project, event, or interface image.

Preferred pattern:

> **Short headline** — one-line explanation of what happened and why it matters. [Source](https://example.com)

### D. Weekly Activity Summary

End every section with a compact summary of the week's overall activity in that market.

Include:

- **Activity level:** Low, Moderate, or High, based on the number and significance of credible developments—not raw post volume alone.
- **Dominant signals:** One or two themes connecting the week's stories.
- **Direction:** A short statement about whether the activity suggests acceleration, continuity, fragmentation, or no meaningful shift.
- **Evidence boundary:** Avoid implying a market-wide conclusion when the available items are sparse, promotional, or concentrated around one event.

Preferred format:

> **Weekly activity — Moderate:** Product activity centered on visual comfort and installation simplification, but there was no clear change in overall competitive direction.

## 4. Editorial Standards

- Write from an internal Acuity perspective. Peerless, A-Light, Eureka, Luminis, Hydrel, Cyclone, Nightingale, Aculux, Gotham, Mark, Lithonia Lighting, and other Acuity portfolio brands are **our brands**, not external competitors or third-party companies.
- When one of our brands has the strongest development, say so naturally: for example, “Our biggest news this week came from Peerless” or “Our Hydrel team earned the section's strongest recognition.”
- Do not overuse “our” in every sentence. Use it where ownership or strategic perspective matters, then name the brand normally.
- Keep competitors, customers, associations, and external design signals in third-person language.

- Write for rapid executive scanning.
- Keep the lead-story paragraph short; keep every secondary item to one line whenever layout permits.
- Lead with what changed, not background information.
- State why an item matters only when the implication is supported by evidence or clearly identified as analysis.
- Prefer original sources: manufacturer announcements, official product pages, design-firm posts, association releases, standards bodies, research institutions, and direct social posts.
- Use reputable trade or general media when it adds independent reporting or when no primary source is available.
- Link to the specific item, not a publication home page or search-results page.
- If one development is relevant to multiple sections, place it where it has the strongest strategic relevance and cross-reference it elsewhere only when necessary.
- Assign each development a canonical story ID and publish it in only one section. If a cross-reference is essential, use a brief pointer to the owning section without repeating the summary, image, or story card.
- Verify every product's actual product type and application against its primary source before assigning a section; a product that does not match the category must be moved or excluded.
- For Customer + Design-Firm Input, use at least three distinct firms whenever three or more firm-related items are included. Do not use one firm for all three sources; disclose a documented limited-source exception when fewer than three qualifying firms are found.
- Do not repeat press-release language or marketing claims as objective fact.
- Do not use weak filler to reach the three-story minimum. Continue the approved gap-search sequence until three credible, correctly labeled stories are verified; if that is genuinely impossible, preserve the prior live edition and report the gap instead of fabricating or publishing internal QA copy.
- Clearly distinguish verified fact from interpretation.
- Do not expose confidential internal strategy or context in report text, citations, search queries, or external tools.

## 5. Story-Priority Test

Choose each section's lead story by weighing:

1. strategic relevance to the covered brand or market;
2. magnitude of the product, technology, channel, customer, or competitive development;
3. potential effect on differentiation, growth, specification, or market direction;
4. credibility and specificity of the evidence;
5. timeliness within the report's stated coverage window.

The most visually appealing story should not automatically become the lead story. Editorial importance comes first; the image supports the decision.

## 6. Links, Images, and Attribution

- Every story or post mentioned must have a working clickable link.
- Use the direct canonical URL whenever available.
- Confirm that links open the intended source before publication.
- Use images only when their source and usage are appropriate for the report.
- Prefer official press, media-kit, product-page, project-page, or social-post images.
- Preserve image credits and captions.
- Do not use a generic stock image when a story-specific image is available.
- Do not let a missing image force a weak story into or a strong story out of the report; use a clearly labeled source image or restrained placeholder only if necessary.

## 7. Visual Direction

- One continuous, responsive website with ten clearly separated scroll sections.
- Simple, clean, minimal, professional, and distinctly architectural.
- Strong section title and clear separation between the lead story and secondary cards.
- Build the page from one large lead-story rectangle and a small number of secondary story rectangles; integrate each image with its text rather than placing a detached photo strip below the reporting.
- Use one dominant lead image plus restrained thumbnails. This is an editorial grid, not a collage.
- Favor whitespace and legibility over dense coverage.
- Use a restrained black, warm-gray, and off-white foundation with one small accent color.
- Use fine rules, a measured modular grid, and crisp architectural typography.
- Introduce subtle sketch or blueprint references through fine construction lines, dimension ticks, crop marks, coordinate labels, or restrained line-art fragments.
- Treat the sketch language as an annotation system, not a themed background; avoid faux-blueprint textures that reduce legibility.
- Avoid heavy gradients, glossy effects, ornamental clutter, and excessive color.
- Keep recurring elements—title, date, source treatment, image credit, and footer—consistent across the site.
- In the site footer, display `Architectural Lighting Weekly` and the edition date in small text.

## 8. Weekly Research and Production Workflow

1. Establish and display the exact reporting window.
2. Review the current `Coverage_Charter.md` before research.
3. Gather candidate items from approved companies, competitors, customers, design firms, associations, events, media, and trend sources.
4. Record the original URL, publication/post date, source, market section, proposed summary, relevance, and image source for every candidate.
5. Remove duplicates, stale items, weak promotional filler, and claims that cannot be verified.
   - Run both a canonical story-ID duplicate scan across sections and a product/category-fit check before drafting.
   - Run the design-firm diversity check before layout.
6. Rank the candidates within each section and select one lead story.
7. Draft the lead paragraph and ultra-short secondary briefs.
8. Extract or select the lead-story image and record its caption and credit.
9. Build the report directly in the approved scrollable website template.
10. Verify every date, name, claim, link, image, caption, and credit.
11. Build and visually inspect the site at desktop and mobile widths for clipping, overlap, inconsistent spacing, weak hierarchy, broken images, and illegible text.
12. Publish the verified web edition to the existing public Site and confirm deployment success.
13. Deliver the report only after content, visual, website, and deployment checks pass.

## 9. Research Source Strategy

Use a layered research model. No single source type is complete enough to drive the report by itself.

### Layer 1 — Direct and Primary Sources

Use these to verify facts and obtain the best story-specific images:

- company newsrooms, product pages, press releases, newsletters, project pages, and media kits;
- design-firm and architecture-firm newsrooms, project portfolios, and research pages;
- association, standards-body, event, award, and conference websites;
- official company, firm, executive, and designer social-media posts.

For consequential claims, prefer the original announcement and corroborate it when independent confirmation would materially improve confidence.

### Layer 2 — Lighting-Industry Editorial Sources

Scan the following every week for news discovery, context, and independent reporting:

- LightNOW;
- EdisonReport;
- Inside Lighting;
- LD+A;
- arc magazine;
- Designing Lighting;
- US Lighting Trends.

Use targeted searches of secondary trade sources when a story affects facilities, electrical channels, building operations, entertainment technology, or another adjacent market.

### Layer 3 — Architecture, Interiors, and Design Editorial Sources

Use these as the architecture/design equivalents of the lighting-industry press:

- ArchDaily;
- Dezeen;
- Architectural Record;
- The Architect's Newspaper;
- Metropolis;
- Interior Design Magazine;
- Hospitality Design;
- Wallpaper\*.

Use them to find project, material, typology, workplace, hospitality, healthcare, public-realm, sustainability, and designer-priority signals—not merely articles that explicitly use the word “lighting.”

### Design Technology + Building Workflow Watchlist

#### Priority Monitoring

- **Autodesk** — Forma, Revit, Dynamo, Autodesk Construction Cloud, AI research, generative design, site and building analysis, and connected design-to-construction workflows.
- **Endra** — Stockholm-based AI platform for MEP design; monitor electrical, lighting, Revit integration, automated calculations, documentation, compliance, and adoption by engineering firms.
- **Finch** — Swedish AI-native building-design platform; monitor firm-encoded design systems, generative layouts, code checking, real-time performance data, and BIM delivery.
- **Motif** — browser-based intelligent workspace founded by former Autodesk/Revit leaders; monitor live Revit/Rhino workflows, architectural AI, collaboration, design review, visualization, and its longer-term BIM ambitions.
- **Snaptrude** — AI-native cloud design workspace spanning brief, programming, site analysis, massing, BIM, collaboration, visualization, and Revit/Rhino exchange.

#### Secondary Monitoring

- **TestFit** — automated site planning and real-estate feasibility; monitor how upstream site, zoning, parking, unit mix, and development decisions constrain later architectural and lighting opportunities.
- **Hypar** — cloud platform for encoding and distributing building-design logic; monitor generative workflows, reusable design functions, automation, and Revit interoperability.
- **Speckle** — AEC data and interoperability layer; monitor the shift from file-based BIM exchange to connected object and data workflows across design tools.
- **Nemetschek Group** — monitor Graphisoft, Vectorworks, Solibri, Bluebeam, dRofus/dTwin, and group-level AI and connected-workflow strategy as the major alternative ecosystem to Autodesk.

Retain companies surfaced through research as candidates until approved for recurring coverage. Reassess the list after the first several reports based on the quality and strategic relevance of the signals produced.

### Layer 4 — Social and Visual Discovery

Use social platforms to detect developments early and capture design signals that may not yet appear in formal media:

- **LinkedIn:** Priority source for launches, appointments, projects, awards, partnerships, events, research, and professional commentary. Review the authenticated network feed when available, plus targeted company and firm pages. Retain a direct post link.
- **Instagram:** Strong visual source for installations, materials, forms, prototypes, exhibitions, emerging studios, and designer reaction.
- **Pinterest:** Useful for aesthetic direction, search behavior, saves, materials, forms, residential influence, and early visual patterns.
- **X / Twitter:** Targeted source for company, journalist, association, event, research, and technology updates; do not treat broad trending topics as lighting-market evidence without validation.

Social-media operating rules:

- Treat social posts as both evidence and discovery cues, depending on the source.
- Verify material product, performance, acquisition, partnership, research, or market claims through a direct or independent source when feasible.
- Link to the specific post, not merely the author profile.
- Exclude promoted posts, generic advice, job advertisements, repetitive reposts, and attractive imagery with no strategic signal.
- Preserve the signed-in session for authenticated-feed work and continue from the last verified position rather than restarting.
- State when an authenticated feed or platform could not be accessed or when the full reporting window was not reached; never represent a partial scan as complete.

### Layer 5 — Search-Interest and Trend Signals

Add a small, disciplined search-intelligence scan to the **Industry Associations + General Trends** section and use it in another section only when directly relevant.

Geographic scope:

- **Primary commercial signal:** United States and Canada.
- **Global design signal:** Maintain a separate worldwide lens for design direction, major international launches, exhibitions, influential projects, materials, forms, and emerging aesthetics.
- Do not combine North American search activity with global design activity into one unlabeled conclusion.

Primary tools and signals:

- **Google Trends:** Compare relative search interest over time by topic, term, geography, and related/rising queries.
- **Pinterest Trends:** Track relative search and save behavior, seasonality, and rising visual/design terms where regional data is available.
- **Search suggestions and related queries:** Use as discovery cues, not as proof of volume.
- **Keyword-volume platforms:** Use Google Keyword Planner or another reputable volume-estimation source only when access and methodology are clear.

Method:

1. Maintain a stable benchmark basket of lighting terms, brand names, product categories, applications, technologies, and design themes.
2. Compare like with like: fixed geography, time window, search type, and topic-versus-term choice.
3. Review week-over-week movement, year-over-year context, related queries, and breakout terms.
4. Add a small rotating basket of new terms discovered through editorial and social research.
5. Report only meaningful movement; do not manufacture a trend from small weekly fluctuations.
6. Label Google Trends and Pinterest Trends values as normalized relative-interest indices, not absolute search counts or market size.
7. Separate consumer/design-interest signals from professional specification or purchase intent.

Suggested recurring term families include:

- architectural lighting, linear lighting, architectural downlighting, decorative lighting, outdoor architectural lighting, healthcare lighting, and behavioral-health lighting;
- human-centric lighting, circadian lighting, biophilic lighting, dark-sky lighting, tunable white, warm dimming, low-voltage lighting, lighting controls, DALI, and wireless lighting controls;
- monitored company and competitor names;
- rising materials, forms, applications, and aesthetic phrases discovered during the weekly scan.

### Source-to-Story Verification Rule

Every candidate item should retain:

- original source and direct URL;
- publication or post date;
- discovery source, if different from the original source;
- applicable report section;
- factual summary;
- strategic relevance;
- confidence level;
- image source and credit;
- whether independent corroboration was completed or needed.

## 10. Recommended Research Operating Model

Use one master research brief each week, executed in distinct passes. This captures most of the benefit of multiple independent scouts without requiring three complete, overlapping research reports every week.

### Pass 1 — Broad Discovery

- Search the approved media, company, firm, association, event, social, and trend sources.
- Build a candidate ledger organized by report section.
- Optimize for recall: find potentially important items without deciding the final report too early.

### Pass 2 — Gap Search + Verification

- Search specifically for weak or empty sections, missed Tier 1 companies, major design firms, and cross-cutting trends.
- Verify dates, original sources, claims, direct links, and image availability.
- Challenge whether each candidate is genuinely new, material, and relevant.
- Remove duplicated press-release rewrites and unsupported promotional claims.

### Pass 3 — Editorial Selection + Synthesis

- Rank stories within each section using the story-priority test.
- Select the lead story and secondary briefs.
- Write the weekly activity synthesis only after reviewing the complete candidate set.
- Check the finished selection for source diversity, geographic balance, and overconcentration on one event or company.

### Authenticated Social Scan

Treat the authenticated LinkedIn network-feed scan as a distinct collection step within the master workflow because it uses a different access surface from open-web research. Integrate its verified candidates into the same ledger. Record whether the complete reporting window was reached.

### Calibration with an Independent Research Report

For the first two or three editions, compare the master workflow against one independently generated research report supplied by Gary or produced through another research source.

Use the comparison to measure:

- important stories found by both;
- credible stories unique to each;
- false positives, stale stories, and weak promotional items;
- source and section gaps;
- link and image quality;
- usefulness of the final strategic implications.

Do not merge the reports automatically. Verify unique items against the report's evidence rules, then update the master prompt, source list, or search terms when the comparison reveals a repeatable gap.

After calibration, use one master workflow for normal weekly production. Run an additional independent scout only for major event weeks, unusually weak coverage, a strategically important launch period, or a periodic quality audit.
